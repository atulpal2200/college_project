Based on **"MCQs Generation With Large Language Models: A Survey of Methodologies, Evolution, and Open Research Issues"** (Anwar et al., 2026), here is the detailed analysis of the literature-review methodology:

---

### **1. Databases and Sources Searched**

- **Academic Libraries:** ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, Scopus, and Semantic Scholar [Section II-A, p. 3].
- **Supplementary Sources:** General-purpose search engines (73 records), AI-based services and recommendation engines (237 records), new papers from review articles (19 records), and forward/backward citation tracking (31 records from references) [Section II-A, p. 3; Figure 2, p. 4].

---

### **2. Publication Period and Reason for Including Older Studies**

- **Publication Period:** **1997 to 2025** [Section II-A, p. 3; Table 2, p. 5].
- **Reason for Older Studies:** To provide an evolutionary perspective tracking how the field progressed from the first dedicated automated MCQ generation system developed by David Coniam in 1997 (rule-based vocabulary cloze tests) through NLP, statistical ML, ontologies, and deep learning, up to modern LLM-based approaches [Section I, p. 2; Section II-A, p. 3; Section III-A, p. 5].

---

### **3. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria**

The authors explicitly outline their eligibility rules in **Table 2** [Section II-A, p. 5]:

- **Inclusion Criteria:**
    
    1. Published between 1997 and 2025.
    2. Written in English.
    3. Primary focus on automated MCQ generation.
    4. Published in top peer-reviewed venues.
    5. Effectiveness empirically demonstrated using multiple metrics.
    6. Introduces novel methods or technical contributions.
- **Exclusion Criteria:**
    
    1. Studies focusing solely on manual creation of MCQs.
    2. Publications written in languages other than English.
    3. Papers addressing general assessment techniques rather than question generation.
    4. Inaccessible or non-peer-reviewed publications.
    5. Duplicated or overlapping studies.
    6. Studies lacking novelty or strong technical contribution.

---

### **4. Record Flow and Study Counts**

- **Initial Identification:** 580 studies collected via top-level title and keyword searches across search engines and recommendation platforms [Section II-A, p. 3].
- **Duplicate Removal:** 201 duplicate records were identified and removed [Section II-A, p. 3; Figure 2, p. 4].
- **Screening & Skimming:** 379 remaining articles underwent content skimming across their abstract, introduction, and methodology sections [Section II-A, p. 3–4].
- **Final Collection Included:** **106 publications** were selected for full systematic review and synthesis [Section II-A, p. 4; Table 1, p. 3; Figure 2, p. 4].
- **Records vs. Unique Studies:** The paper **does not explicitly distinguish** between individual paper records and unique underlying studies; it treats each of the 106 included publications as a distinct study entry [Table 1, p. 3; Table 3, p. 4; Figure 3b, p. 5].

---

### **5. Systematic Review Protocol**

- The authors describe their process as a _"systematic literature review methodology"_ designed to provide an unbiased overview [Section II, p. 3].
- **Protocol Citation:** The paper **does not explicitly cite a named, standard systematic review protocol** (such as PRISMA or Kitchenham guidelines). It references a citation expansion strategy (Khalid & Wu, 2020) and a thematic analysis framework (Bano et al., 2023) [Section II-A–B, p. 3–4].

---

### **6. Study Data Extraction and Classification**

- **Data Extraction:** Extracted data points included methodology, application domain, datasets used, evaluation metrics, key results, and reported limitations. Extracted items were re-verified to eliminate factual errors or contextual mismatches [Section II-B, p. 4].
- **Thematic Synthesis:** Synthesized using the _Thematic Analysis and Categorization Schema_ to map major research trends and gaps [Section II-B, p. 4].
- **Methodological Taxonomy:** Included studies were classified into **8 primary methodology categories** [Table 3, p. 4; Figure 3b, p. 5]:
    1. **Natural Language Processing (NLP):** 20 studies
    2. **Hybrid Approaches:** 12 studies
    3. **Machine Learning (ML):** 11 studies
    4. **Ontology-Based:** 11 studies
    5. **Rule-Based:** 10 studies
    6. **Deep Learning (DL):** 6 studies
    7. **Template-Based:** 3 studies
    8. **Large Language Models (LLMs):** 33 studies
        - _LLM Sub-categories:_ Prompting LLMs (9), Fine-Tuned LLMs (7), Pipeline/Modular (7), Hybrid LLMs (5), and Multi-Agent LLMs (5) [Figure 3b, p. 5].

---

### **7. Methodological Limitations and Potential Biases**

The authors explicitly acknowledge three main limitations in **Section II-C** (_"Limitations of the Survey"_, p. 5):

1. **Search Coverage Bias:** The search strategy may have missed relevant studies published in languages other than English or hosted in less accessible journals.
2. **Classification Subjectivity:** Categorizing studies into discrete taxonomy buckets involves inherent subjectivity because many systems employ hybrid combinations of multiple techniques.
3. **Temporal Rapid-Evolution Snapshot:** Due to the extremely rapid pace of advancements in LLMs, the survey represents a snapshot of the literature at the time of writing, and newer developments will quickly emerge.

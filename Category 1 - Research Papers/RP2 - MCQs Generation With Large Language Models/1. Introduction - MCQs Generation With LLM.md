The Introduction of **"MCQs Generation With Large Language Models: A Survey of Methodologies, Evolution, and Open Research Issues"** (Anwar et al., 2026) lays the foundation for understanding how Multiple Choice Question (MCQ) generation has transformed over time.

Multiple-Choice Questions are a staple in educational testing, standardized exams (like the SAT or GRE), and professional certifications because they are **objective, efficient, and easy to score at scale**. However, creating good MCQs manually is a massive burden for educators—it requires deep domain knowledge, pedagogical skills, and hours of writing to create plausible incorrect options (distractors) without introducing ambiguity or errors.

To solve this, researchers initially built **rule-based systems** (starting with David Coniam in 1997), but these were rigid and hard to scale. The field subsequently evolved through statistical models, domain ontologies, and deep learning architectures. The release of the **Transformer architecture in 2017** and modern **Large Language Models (LLMs)** (such as ChatGPT, GPT-4, and Gemini) created a major shift, allowing systems to produce fluent, context-aware questions from diverse text sources with minimal human effort.

Despite these advances, the authors point out that evaluating question quality remains a major challenge. Traditional automated text-similarity metrics (like BLEU or ROUGE) measure word overlap rather than educational value or conceptual correctness, making human review and domain adaptation (such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation / RAG) essential. Earlier literature reviews summarized older multi-step workflows or focused narrowly on specific fields (like medicine) without providing a comprehensive overview of how LLM-driven generation compares to classical techniques. This survey fills that gap by analyzing **106 studies published between 1997 and 2025**.

---

### **1. What problem are the authors trying to address?**

The authors address **two interconnected problems**:

1. **The Manual MCQ Bottleneck:** Constructing high-quality MCQs manually is laborious, time-consuming, and expensive. Test writers must ensure factual accuracy, syllabus alignment, and pedagogical validity while crafting distractors that reflect real student misconceptions rather than obvious mistakes.
2. **Fragmented Literature & Lack of Evaluation Consensus:** Although automated generation reduces human workload, recent research using LLMs has developed rapidly and fragmentedly across different domains. Earlier literature reviews failed to capture the full evolution from rule-based models to LLMs or provide a unified framework for open challenges, evaluation standards, and system architectures in the LLM era. _(Section I: Introduction, pp. 1–3)_

---

### **2. Why is automatic MCQ generation useful?**

Automatic MCQ generation reduces the time, cost, and human labor required to create assessment items. It is critical for:

- **Computer-Based & Adaptive Testing (CBT/CAT):** Providing the massive, continuous supply of test items required so question banks do not run out or leak.
- **Scalable E-Learning & MOOCs:** Enabling platforms like Coursera or edX to deliver automated practice quizzes and immediate feedback to thousands of students simultaneously.
- **Personalized Learning:** Allowing systems to dynamically tailor question difficulty to individual student skill levels.
- **Standardized & Professional Certification:** Streamlining test assembly for high-stakes fields like medicine, nursing, engineering, and language learning. _(Section I: Introduction, pp. 1–3; Section IX: Applications, pp. 13–15)_

---

### **3. What limitations of earlier approaches motivated the evolution toward LLM-based methods?**

The paper outlines how limitations across previous generations of systems drove the adoption of LLMs:

- **Rule-Based & Template-Based Systems (1990s–2000s):** Relying on handcrafted grammar rules or fixed sentence slots made these systems rigid, brittle, domain-bound, and incapable of producing varied or linguistically complex phrasing.
- **Statistical & Classical Machine Learning (2000s–2010s):** Using shallow parsing, POS tags, and TF-IDF required heavy manual feature engineering and struggled with deep semantic or contextual understanding.
- **Ontology-Based Approaches:** Depend heavily on formal, structured knowledge bases (OWL/RDF) that are expensive and labor-intensive to build, while struggling with natural sentence verbalisation.
- **Early Deep Learning (RNNs, LSTMs, early T5/BERT):** Improved sentence fluency but depended heavily on large annotated datasets, suffered from limited context windows, and lacked strong multi-step reasoning capabilities.
- **The LLM Paradigm Shift:** Transformers and LLMs solved these issues by leveraging pre-training on massive datasets, enabling strong in-context learning, prompt-driven adaptation, multi-modal generation, and natural linguistic fluency. _(Section I: Introduction, p. 2; Section III: Evolution, pp. 5–6; Section V: Classification, pp. 6–13)_

---

### **4. What are the paper's three research questions?**

In Section I (_Introduction_, p. 3), the authors define **three core research questions (RQs)**:

1. **RQ1:** _"How have MCQs generation methods developed from simple rule-based systems to modern LLMs-based techniques?"_
2. **RQ2:** _"What are the main strategies used by LLMs systems to generate good-quality MCQs automatically?"_
3. **RQ3:** _"What open problems and opportunities remain in this area of LLMs-based MCQs generation?"_

---

### **5. What are the paper's main contributions?**

In Section I (_Introduction_, p. 3), the authors explicitly list **nine main contributions** of their survey:

1. **Historical Evolution:** Tracing the transition of MCQ generation from early rule-based systems to modern LLM architectures.
2. **Theoretical Foundations:** Explaining MCQ structure (stem, key, distractors) and psychometric principles across domains.
3. **Methodological Taxonomy:** Classifying techniques into rule-based, template-driven, ontology-based, ML/DL, LLM-based, and hybrid categories.
4. **Generic System Architecture:** Abstracting a 3-phase framework (Content Identification, Item Construction, Quality Assurance).
5. **Evaluation Review:** Analyzing automatic metrics, human review rubrics, and psychometric metrics with their respective trade-offs.
6. **Dataset Mapping:** Cataloging key public datasets (size, domain, answer type, and coverage) used for training/benchmarking.
7. **Application Synthesis:** Surveying real-world applications across education, language testing, medicine, and corporate training.
8. **LLM Analysis:** Evaluating specific strengths and weaknesses of LLMs (hallucinations, distractor quality, prompt strategies, multi-agent frameworks).
9. **Research Roadmap:** Identifying open challenges and future directions (adaptive learning, multi-modal QG, ethical mitigation, human-AI collaboration).

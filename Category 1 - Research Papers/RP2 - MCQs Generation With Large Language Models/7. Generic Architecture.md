Section VII, **“Generic Architecture”** (pp. 12–13, Figure 7) of _MCQs Generation With Large Language Models: A Survey of Methodologies, Evolution, and Open Research Issues_ (Anwar et al., 2026) abstracts the automated MCQ generation process into a **three-phase sequential pipeline** inspired by human authoring workflows.

---

### **1. Overall Pipeline Architecture**

As shown in **Figure 7** (p. 13), the generic system architecture processes raw unstructured text through three distinct, sequential phases:

1. **Phase I: Content Identification and Selection**
2. **Phase II: Item Construction**
3. **Phase III: Quality Assurance and Refinement**

---

### **2. Stage-by-Stage Explanation**

#### **Phase I: Content Identification and Selection Phase** (p. 12)

- **Step 1: Pre-Processing**
    - **Purpose:** Clean, normalize, and structure raw input text.
    - **Input:** Raw unstructured source text.
    - **Processing:** Sentence simplification, Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging, syntactic parsing, co-reference resolution, and Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD).
    - **Output:** Cleaned, normalized text annotated with syntactic and semantic tags.
- **Step 2: Sentence Selection**
    - **Purpose:** Identify candidate sentences suitable for question generation.
    - **Input:** Pre-processed text.
    - **Processing:** Evaluates sentences based on sentence length, syntactic simplicity, and Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) patterns; Machine Learning (ML) or Deep Learning (DL) models rank candidate sentences.
    - **Output:** Ranked candidate sentences.
- **Step 3: Key Selection**
    - **Purpose:** Identify the target correct answer term ("key") within a chosen sentence.
    - **Input:** Candidate sentence.
    - **Processing:** Uses frequency metrics (TF-IDF), syntactic cues (POS/parse information), or ML models predicting salient answer phrases.
    - **Output:** Identified target key / answer entity.

#### **Phase II: Item Construction Phase** (pp. 12–13)

- **Step 1: Question Generation**
    - **Purpose:** Transform the selected sentence and key into an interrogative question stem.
    - **Input:** Selected sentence and target key.
    - **Processing:** Replaces the key with an appropriate _wh_-word (_who, what, where_) based on semantic type, or rephrases sentence structure using dependency patterns or SVO rules.
    - **Output:** Question stem.
- **Step 2: Distractor Generation**
    - **Purpose:** Create plausible yet incorrect answer choices.
    - **Input:** Target key and sentence context.
    - **Processing:** Leverages lexical resources (WordNet synonyms), domain ontologies, distributional hypothesis (contextually similar words), or POS matching for grammatical consistency (Table 21, p. 14).
    - **Output:** Set of candidate distractors.

#### **Phase III: Quality Assurance and Refinement Phase** (p. 13)

- **Step 1: Verification**
    - **Purpose:** Perform automated and semi-automated quality checks on draft MCQ items.
    - **Input:** Draft MCQ items (stem, key, distractors).
    - **Processing:** Grammar/syntax validation, semantic consistency checks (Named Entity Recognition [NER], Semantic Role Labeling [SRL]), similarity metrics for distractor plausibility, and predictive difficulty models.
    - **Output:** Verified / scored MCQ items.
- **Step 2: Post-Processing**
    - **Purpose:** Refine, filter, and finalize items for deployment.
    - **Input:** Verified MCQ items.
    - **Processing:** Rewording awkward stems, enhancing distractor quality, discarding failing items, human subject-matter expert review (checking pedagogical soundness & factual correctness), ordering items by difficulty, formatting for delivery, and optional pilot testing.
    - **Output:** Final assessment items ready for exam deployment.

---

### **3. Key Selection and Distractor Generation Details**

- **Key Selection:** Identified in Phase I using TF-IDF term frequency, POS tagging, syntactic parse trees, or ML models trained to predict salient answer phrases (p. 12).
- **Distractor Generation:** Constructed in Phase II by drawing candidate terms from lexical databases (WordNet), domain ontologies, contextually similar corpus words (distributional hypothesis), or POS matching to ensure grammatical agreement with the key (Table 21, p. 14).

---

### **4. LLM Integration in the Architecture**

Across Sections V-E and VII (pp. 9–13), the paper notes that Large Language Models (LLMs) fit into this generic architecture in two main ways:

1. **Modular Integration:** Serving as specialized components within specific pipeline stages (e.g., using BERT for key/sentence selection, GPT for question generation, or an LLM-as-a-Judge for Phase III verification).
2. **End-to-End Consolidation:** Consolidating Phase I (content selection) and Phase II (stem and distractor construction) into unified prompt execution workflows (such as RAG, Chain-of-Thought, or Multi-Agent frameworks) where the LLM performs content selection and item generation within in-context memory.

---

### **5. Quality Control Mechanisms**

Quality control is explicitly embedded in **Phase III: Quality Assurance and Refinement** (p. 13):

- **Automated Quality Checks:** Grammar/syntax checkers, semantic consistency validation (NER, SRL), similarity metrics to prevent duplicate/obvious distractors, and difficulty prediction models.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Refinement:** Subject-matter expert review to verify factual correctness and educational validity, discarding failing items, and optional pilot-testing with learners.

### **7. Limitations and Challenges Explicitly Discussed**

- **Automated Verification Blind Spots:** Automated tools (grammar checkers, similarity metrics) miss subtle conceptual errors, factual inaccuracies, or logical fallacies, making human expert verification necessary (p. 13).
- **Dependence on Expert Review:** System throughput remains bottlenecked by human review because fully automated quality assurance is not yet reliable (p. 13).
- **Sequential Error Cascading:** Flaws in early stages (e.g., selecting an incomplete sentence in Phase I) cascade downward, producing ungrammatical stems or non-plausible distractors in Phase II (pp. 12–13).
- **Inter-Rater Agreement Variance:** Human evaluation during post-processing suffers from variance in inter-rater agreement even when explicit rubrics are provided (p. 13; Section VIII-B, p. 14).

---

### **Five Technical Takeaways for Our Project**

1. **RAG Replaces Classical Phase I Feature Engineering:** RAG vector search and chunk retrieval consolidate classical NLP preprocessing, sentence selection, and key extraction into a single, document-grounded context fetch.
2. **Multi-Stage Prompts Mirror Phase II Pipeline:** To prevent superficial LLM distractors, structure prompts in explicit sub-steps (1. Extract key fact → 2. Draft stem → 3. Generate distractors targeting common misconceptions) matching Phase II.
3. **Automated Verification Gates Prevent Garbage Input to Professors:** Implement Phase III automated verification (checking stem-key document overlap, option duplication, and JSON format) _before_ rendering items in the professor dashboard.
4. **Professor Review Fills Phase III Verification Blind Spots:** The professor review interface (_Approve / Edit / Reject_) serves as the essential human-in-the-loop expert review mandated in Phase III to catch subtle factual hallucinations.
5. **Prevent Sequential Error Cascading:** If RAG retrieves a fragmented PDF chunk (e.g., an incomplete sentence or diagram caption), the LLM will generate a flawed MCQ. Pre-filtering poor PDF text chunks during RAG ingestion is essential.

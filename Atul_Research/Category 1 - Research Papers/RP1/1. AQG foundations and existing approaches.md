# Research Paper 1
## Paper's Purpose
### **1. Why the Authors Conducted This Review (Motivation)**

The authors—Ghader Kurdi, Jared Leo, Bijan Parsia, Uli Sattler, and Salam Al-Emari—conducted this systematic literature review for several key reasons:

- **High Cost of Manual Construction:** Constructing exam-style questions manually is a complex, resource-intensive task requiring specialized training, experience, and time. Analyses of actual exam questions frequently show poor item quality due to a lack of formal training in assessment development.
- **Rising Demand for Question Pools:** Questions lose their assessment value once shared among test-takers, requiring continuous replacement. Furthermore, modern e-learning technologies—such as Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), adaptive testing, and drill-and-practice exercises—require a massive, continuous supply of test questions.
- **Rapid Expansion of Research:** The paper extends a previous benchmark systematic review by Alsubait (2015), which covered literature published up to late 2014. Between 2015 and early 2019, 93 new papers (representing 72 distinct studies) were published—more than was produced in the preceding 45 years combined.
- **Verifying Persistent Gaps:** The authors wanted to investigate whether earlier limitations highlighted by Alsubait (2015)—such as poor question quality, uncontrolled question difficulty, and weak evaluation standards—had been addressed by recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Semantic Web technologies.

---

### **2. The Problem Investigated**

The central problem investigated in the paper is the **technological and educational bottleneck in Automatic Question Generation (AQG)**.

AQG refers to constructing algorithms that produce questions automatically from knowledge sources, whether structured (e.g., ontologies, databases) or unstructured (e.g., text). The authors investigated whether current AQG systems successfully fulfill real-world educational demands, focusing specifically on five core challenges:

1. **Controlling Question Difficulty:** Predicting and manipulating how easy or hard a question will be.
2. **Enriching Question Forms and Structures(Difficulty level of Questions):** Moving beyond simple surface-level questions toward higher cognitive levels (e.g., Bloom's taxonomy).
3. **Automating Template Construction:** Reducing the manual effort required to design question templates.
4. **Improving Presentation (Verbalisation):** Ensuring generated questions are grammatically correct, fluent, and naturally phrased.
5. **Generating Feedback:** Providing meaningful explanations to learners regarding why an answer is correct or incorrect.

---

### **3. Research Objectives**

In Section _"Review Objective"_ (pp. 125–126), the authors structure their systematic review around **four primary research objectives (OBJ1–OBJ4)**:

- **OBJ1: Providing an overview of the AQG community and its activities**
    - Tracking publication rates, paper types (conferences, journals, workshops), publication venues, and active research groups.
- **OBJ2: Summarising current AQG approaches**
    - Categorizing generation purpose, generation methods (**syntax-based**, **semantic-based**, **template-based**, **rule-based**, **statistical methods**), inputs, target domains (e.g., language learning, medicine, computer science), question formats (e.g., **factual wh-questions**, **gap-fill / cloze questions**), response formats (**Multiple Choice / MC**, **Free Response / FR**), feedback generation, difficulty control, and verbalisation.
- **OBJ3: Identifying the gold-standard performance in AQG**
    - Examining available standard datasets (e.g., SQuAD, NewsQA, SciQ, QGSTEC), evaluation types (expert review, mock exams, student testing, crowdsourcing), evaluated question properties, and measurement metrics.
- **OBJ4: Tracking the evolution of AQG since Alsubait's review**
    - Measuring specific progress on feedback generation, controlled difficulty, and naturalness/verbalisation since 2014.

---

### **4. Contributions of the Paper**

In Section 1 (_"Introduction"_, p. 122), the authors explicitly define **four main contributions** of their work to the research community:

1. **A Comprehensive Summary:** Delivering a detailed synthesis of recent AQG approaches published between 2015 and early 2019.
2. **Comparative State of the Field:** Providing a structural analysis highlighting key differences and shifts between the pre-2014 and post-2014 research periods.
3. **Challenges and Future Roadmap:** Outlining persistent limitations and setting actionable directions for future system design, template creation, and difficulty modeling.
4. **An Extensive Reference Guide:** Mapping out active research groups, top publication venues, and standard evaluation datasets for future researchers.

---

### **5. Key Findings of the Authors**

Across Sections _"Results and Discussion"_ and _"Conclusion and Future Work"_ (pp. 131–160), the authors outline several core findings:

- **Primary Purpose:** Assessment remains the primary goal of AQG (accounting for 78.5% of studies pre-2014 and 40 out of 72 studies post-2014).
- **Domain & Question Prevailing Trends:** Language learning remains the most researched domain, with simple factual **wh-questions** and **gap-fill (cloze) questions** being the most prevalent formats.
- **Persistent Educational Deficits:** Despite the growth in publication volume, minimal progress has been made on controlling difficulty (only 14 studies addressed it), verbalisation (only 10 studies), or generating educational feedback (only 1 study generated feedback alongside questions).
- **Evaluation Bottleneck:** Direct comparison across AQG approaches is currently impossible due to extreme heterogeneity in evaluation metrics, unstandardized rating scales, and unsatisfactory experimental reporting (e.g., missing participant details, missing sample size justifications, and uncalculated inter-rater reliability).

    - All statements above regarding publication stats (93 papers / 72 studies), percentages of question types, identified objectives (OBJ1–OBJ4), and listed gaps (difficulty control, feedback, evaluation heterogeneity) are direct empirical findings reported explicitly by Kurdi et al..

## Methodology
The paper **"A Systematic Review of Automatic Question Generation for Educational Purposes"** (Kurdi et al., 2020) employs a systematic literature review (SLR) methodology following the established guidelines of **Kitchenham and Charters (2007)** and **Boland et al. (2013)**.

Below is an in-depth breakdown of each component of the authors' review methodology, including the search process, selection criteria, data analysis, and the rationale behind their choices.

---

### **1. Search Period & Sources**

- **Search Period:** The review covers literature published between **2015 and early 2019** (with the final search update performed on **March 4, 2019**). This timeframe extends the previous benchmark SLR by Alsubait (2015), which reviewed AQG literature up to late 2014.
- **Primary Data Sources:** The authors searched **six primary data sources**:
    - **Five computerized databases:** ERIC, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, INSPEC, and ScienceDirect.
    - **Targeted educational AI venues:** The _International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education_ (AIED) journal and the proceedings of the _International Conference on Artificial Intelligence in Education_ for 2015, 2017, and 2018.
- **Supplementary Search Strategies:**
    - **Snowballing & Citation Tracking:** To capture relevant papers missed by electronic database queries, the authors examined reference lists of reviewed papers and tracked forward citations using Google Scholar's "cited by" feature for every identified AQG paper.
    - **Sanity Checks:** The authors cross-checked their search results against two concurrent AQG reviews (Ch & Saha, 2018; Papasalouros & Chatzigiannakou, 2018) to evaluate search comprehensiveness.

---

### **2. Search Queries & Execution**

- **Keywords & Logic:** The core search query combined the keywords `"question"` and `"generation"` using database-specific syntax (e.g., proximity operators such as `NEAR/5` in IEEE/INSPEC or exact phrase searches in ScienceDirect and ERIC).
- **Pragmatic Search Capping:** For large databases yielding high volumes of non-relevant hits (such as ACM with 1,265 results), the authors inspected the top **200 results sorted by relevance**, as subsequent results degraded significantly in precision. In total, **974 database records** were individually checked.

---

### **3. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria**

The study **included** all research tackling automatic question generation specifically for **educational purposes** (such as tutoring systems, student assessment, and self-directed practice), without restricting domain or question format.

To maintain high rigor, the authors applied **13 explicit exclusion criteria** (Criteria 1–5 adapted from Alsubait 2015; Criteria 6–13 added by Kurdi et al.). A paper was excluded if:

1. It was not written in English.
2. It presented work-in-progress without a sufficient description of the QG approach.
3. The generator relied merely on slot substitution with numerals or predefined random values.
4. It focused primarily on Question Answering (QA) rather than Question Generation.
5. It focused on assessment delivery mechanisms rather than generating questions.
6. It focused on assembling exams or adaptively selecting questions from existing question banks.
7. It predicted the difficulty of human-authored questions.
8. It generated questions for non-educational purposes (e.g., training dialogue systems or QA models).
9. It omitted an empirical evaluation of the generated questions.
10. It was an unmodifed extension of a pre-2015 study.
11. It was a secondary study (literature review).
12. It was not peer-reviewed (e.g., dissertations, technical reports, slides).
13. Its full text was inaccessible.

---

### **4. Screening Process**

1. **Title and Abstract Screening:** Exported search results were screened independently by **two reviewers** looking at titles and abstracts. Ambiguous papers were skimmed to determine eligibility.
2. **Educational Purpose Verification:** Purpose was judged by reviewing the title, abstract, introduction, and conclusion. Papers listing multiple generic applications without designating education as a primary focus were excluded.
3. **Full-Text Evaluation & Disagreement Resolution:** Final decisions on criteria requiring full-text reading (e.g., verifying question generation mechanisms and evaluation details) were made after complete text analysis. Inter-reviewer agreement was checked, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

---

### **5. Number of Included Studies & Paper Grouping**

- **Database Yield:** Initial searches yielded 2,012 raw hits, of which 974 were checked. Screening titles/abstracts identified 122 relevant papers (89 after deduplication), which were reduced to **36 included papers** following full-text evaluation.
- **Snowballing & Citation Tracking Yield:** Supplementary searches yielded **57 additional unique included papers**.
- **Total Paper Count:** **93 final papers** met all inclusion criteria.
- **Grouping into Distinct Studies:** To prevent distorting statistical trends, multiple papers by shared authors were consolidated into a single study entry if they reported on:
    1. Different evaluations of the exact same generator.
    2. The same approach applied across different input sources or domains.
    3. Minor incremental features (e.g., adding distractor generation) without altering the underlying core generation process.
- **Total Study Count:** The 93 papers represented **72 distinct AQG studies**.

---

### **6. Data Extraction, Synthesis, and Quality Assessment**

- **Data Extraction:** Guided by a predefined extraction form mapped directly to research objectives OBJ1–OBJ3, two reviewers independently extracted data from each study. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.
- **Data Synthesis:** Extracted data were compiled, classified, and analyzed programmatically using custom **R Markdown code** (published publicly on GitHub).
- **Methodological Quality Assessment (QA):** To evaluate the empirical strength of the included studies, the authors assessed each evaluation approach against a **9-criterion checklist (Q1–Q9)** adapted from established health and clinical appraisal tools (Downs & Black 1998; Reisch et al. 1989; CASP 2018):
    - _Participants (Q1–Q4):_ Reporting participant counts, demographic characteristics/suitability, recruitment procedures, and overall task appropriateness.
    - _Question Samples (Q5–Q7):_ Reporting question sample sizes, sampling strategy/justifications (e.g., random vs. stratified), and sample representativeness.
    - _Measurement Rigor (Q8–Q9):_ Providing explicit definitions/formulas for metrics and calculating inter-rater reliability (e.g., Cohen's kappa).
- **QA Reliability:** Inter-rater reliability for the QA checklist was pre-tested on an exploratory sample of 27 studies by two reviewers, achieving **73% to 100% raw agreement** and **Cohen's kappa > 0.72** across criteria Q1–Q5.

---

### **7. Why Each Methodological Choice Matters**

|Methodological Choice|Practical & Research Significance|
|:--|:--|
|**Kitchenham & Charters Guidelines**|Guarantees a reproducible, standardized, and unbiased review process that minimizes researcher selection bias.|
|**Combination of Computerized Databases & Targeted AIED Venues**|Ensures multi-disciplinary coverage spanning computer science, natural language processing, semantic web, and educational technology.|
|**Snowballing & Citation Tracking**|**Crucial for coverage:** Database keyword searches alone captured only 36 of the final 93 papers. Citation tracking uncovered **57 additional papers** (over 61% of the total corpus), proving that keyword searches miss significant portions of AQG literature.|
|**Strict Peer-Review & Evaluation Exclusion (Criteria 9 & 12)**|Filters out unverified technical claims, ensuring the synthesis reflects validated algorithms with empirical proof of question quality.|
|**Consolidating Papers into Distinct Studies (93 papers → 72 studies)**|Prevents overcounting and inflation of research trends caused by research groups publishing multiple incremental conference papers on a single generator.|
|**Independent Dual-Reviewer Screening & Extraction**|Reduces subjective bias in evaluating paper relevance and ensures data extraction fidelity.|
|**R Markdown Code Execution**|Enhances research transparency and reproducibility by automating data synthesis and making the analysis code open-source.|
|**Dedicated Quality Assessment Checklist (Q1–Q9)**|Exposes widespread methodological weaknesses in the field—such as missing sample size justifications, uncalculated inter-rater reliability, and vague reviewer instructions—explaining why AQG results are currently difficult to compare across studies.|

# Main Findings
The major findings and conclusions of the systematic literature review **"A Systematic Review of Automatic Question Generation for Educational Purposes"** (Kurdi et al., 2020) are organized below according to the requested categories. All findings are drawn directly from the paper.

---

### **1. Research Trends & Community Activity**

- **Surge in Publication Volume:** Research in educational Automatic Question Generation (AQG) experienced a dramatic expansion, with 93 papers published between 2015 and early 2019 compared to 81 papers over the preceding 45 years combined (1970–2014).
- **Drivers of Growth:** This acceleration coincides with the launch of major MOOC platforms (Coursera, edX, Udacity in 2012), dedicated QG workshops (2008–2010), and the maturity of modern Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Semantic Web tools.
- **Publication Venues & Active Groups:** Conference papers constitute the majority of publications (44 papers), followed by journal articles (32 papers) and workshop papers (17 papers). The review identified 358 researchers across the field, with 13 core research groups producing 64 of the 174 total publications analyzed across both review periods.
- **Primary Purpose:** Student assessment remains the predominant purpose of generated questions (40 out of 72 distinct studies), followed by general education (10 studies), self-directed learning (9 studies), learning support (9 studies), and providing practice exercises (8 studies). Most systems are evaluated in experimental settings; only one study (Zavala & Mendoza, 2018) reported actual deployment in a classroom setting.

---

### **2. Question-Generation Approaches**

- **Level of Understanding Required:**
    - **Semantic-based (60 papers):** The vast majority of approaches require a deeper understanding beyond surface lexical features, utilizing ontologies, taxonomies, feature-based similarity, or contextual embeddings to guide question and distractor selection.
    - **Syntax-based (10 papers):** A small subset operates solely on syntactic structures (e.g., Part-of-Speech tags, dependency parse trees), with nearly all focused on language learning.
- **Procedure of Transformation:**
    - **Template-based (27 papers):** The most popular transformation approach; templates define surface structures with placeholders, allowing questions to differ from the input text but limiting linguistic diversity.
    - **Rule-based (16 papers):** Matches syntactic or semantic annotations to handcrafted patterns to transform assertive sentences into interrogative forms.
    - **Statistical / Data-driven methods (9 papers):** Treats question generation as sequence-to-sequence prediction or uses machine learning models to select gap positions.
- **Generation Pipeline Tasks:** The generation workflow is structured into **preprocessing** (sentence simplification, sentence classification, content selection) [61–64], **question construction** (stem/answer generation, distractor selection based on similarity or knowledge graph query relaxation) [68–70], and **post-processing** (verbalisation, over-generate and rank filtering).

---

### **3. Input Sources**

- **Unstructured Text:** Text remains the most popular input format (42 studies), primarily used for generating language learning questions (18 studies) or simple factual questions (16 studies).
- **Structured Knowledge Sources (Semantic Web):** Ontologies (8 studies) and Resource Description Framework (RDF) knowledge bases (6 studies) are second. These inputs are heavily associated with generating Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs) (10 studies vs. 4 for free-response) and support broader domain variety.
- **Question Stems & Keys:** 5 studies utilize pre-existing question stems and correct keys as inputs specifically to generate distractors.

---

### **4. Question Types, Formats, Domains & Languages**

- **Question Formats:** Gap-fill (cloze) questions and simple factual _wh_-questions (_what_, _where_, _who_, _when_) remain the most frequently generated formats because they require relatively little construction effort and avoid complex surface-structure verbalisation issues.
- **Response Formats:** Selected-response / MCQs (35 studies) and Free Response (32 studies) are almost equally investigated, while 4 studies address both.
- **Target Domains:** Language learning is by far the most investigated domain (21 studies), driven by standardized language tests (TOEFL, IELTS), abundant reading passages, and mature POS taggers. Other domains include Medicine/Biomedicine (4 studies), Mathematics (4 studies), Computer Science (3 studies), Physics (1), Geometry (1), Biology (1), History (1), and Sport (1) [77, 85–88].
- **Languages:** 59 studies focus on English. Non-English generation is growing, including Chinese (5 studies), Japanese (3 studies), Indonesian (2 studies), Punjabi (1 study), and Thai (1 study).

---

### **5. Evaluation Practices & Performance**

- **Evaluation Methods:** Expert review is the most common evaluation method (21 studies), followed by automated metrics against human reference questions (15 studies), student testing/mock exams (10 for testing, 9 for reviewing), and crowdsourcing via Mechanical Turk (10 studies) [112, 114–116].
- **Standard Datasets & Automated Metrics:** Machine reading comprehension corpora (SQuAD, NewsQA) and QA datasets (SciQ, 30M Factoid, QGSTEC) are increasingly used [103–108, 110]. Automated metrics such as ROUGE (6 studies), BLEU (5 studies), and METEOR (3 studies) measure n-gram overlap with reference questions. Human evaluations focus on acceptability (17), grammatical correctness (14), semantic ambiguity (11), educational usefulness (10), distractor plausibility (16), and empirical difficulty/discrimination (19).
- **Incomparability & Quality Flaws:** Direct performance comparisons across AQG approaches are impossible due to extreme measurement heterogeneity (e.g., varying rating scale sizes) and widespread reporting flaws. Methodological Quality Assessment (Q1–Q9 checklist) revealed that studies frequently omit participant counts/demographics, sample size justifications, sampling strategies, and inter-rater reliability (missing in 43 studies).

---

### **6. Difficulty Control**

- **Current State:** Difficulty control is severely neglected across the literature; only 14 studies addressed controlling or predicting question difficulty.
- **Features & Validation:** 8 studies focus on domain-specific difficulty (e.g., math word problems, DFA problems, code tracing), while 6 focus on generic difficulty. Proposed features include key-distractor similarity, passage readability, number/type of domain rules, predicate popularity, and concept depth in class hierarchies [91–94]. Models are validated via expert predictions or student test performance, but sample sizes are typically small (under 100 questions) [89, 95–97].

---

### **7. Feedback Generation**

- **Current State:** Feedback generation is almost non-existent in both historical and recent AQG literature.
- **Findings:** Only **one study** out of 72 (Leo et al., 2019) generated pedagogical feedback alongside questions, verbalizing the underlying ontology axioms used to select keys and distractors. One other study (Das & Majumder, 2017) generated syntactic hints.

---

### **8. Identified Research Gaps & Future Directions**

- **Question-Level Improvements:**
    1. **Controlled Difficulty Models:** Combine stem and option features into holistic difficulty models rather than evaluating options in isolation.
    2. **Enriching Forms & Cognitive Depth:** Move beyond surface-level factual questions toward higher-order cognitive levels (e.g., Bloom’s taxonomy).
    3. **Automating Template Construction:** Automate template extraction to eliminate expensive manual expert design and increase structural diversity.
    4. **NLG & Verbalisation:** Employ natural language generation to eliminate grammatical clues and produce natural paraphrases.
    5. **Pedagogical Feedback:** Integrate formative, summative, and personalized feedback generation into question generators.
- **Evaluation & Reporting Standards:**
    1. **Leveraging Real Exams:** Translate human-authored exam questions into machine-processable representations to benchmark generator quality and difficulty models.
    2. **Harmonized Scoring & Automation:** Establish standard scoring procedures and automatic evaluation metrics that correlate with human judgment.
    3. **Mandatory Reporting Checklists:** Enforce experimental reporting checklists (covering sample size justifications, participant demographics, and inter-rater reliability) prior to peer review.
- **Systemic Directions:** Move from single-question generation to assembling balanced exams; mine real-world exams to align generation with educational needs; optimize fast semantic similarity measures; and enrich domain ontologies for QG.

# AQG Approaches
In their systematic literature review, **Kurdi et al. (2020)** extend and reorganize previous AQG classifications (such as Yao et al., 2012, and Alsubait, 2015) by categorizing question-generation approaches along **two primary dimensions**: the **level of input understanding required** and the **procedure used to transform input into questions**.

Below is an in-depth explanation of each approach using the paper's taxonomy and terminology [55–58].

---

### **1. Dimension 1: Level of Input Understanding Required**

#### **A. Syntactic / Syntax-Based Approaches**

- **How It Works:** Operates strictly on the surface-level syntax of the input (e.g., Part-of-Speech [POS] tags or parse-tree dependency relations) to guide question generation. It does not require understanding the underlying semantics or real-world entities mentioned in the text.
- **Required Inputs:** Unstructured text accompanied by shallow Natural Language Processing (NLP) annotations (e.g., POS taggers, dependency parsers).
- **Strengths:** Computationally straightforward and inexpensive to implement; highly effective for simple language learning assessments (e.g., vocabulary or grammar testing) where shallow text processing is sufficient.
- **Limitations:** Cannot capture conceptual relationships or semantic meaning. When applying syntactic transformations to convert assertive sentences into questions, these approaches frequently yield ungrammatical, nonsensical, or awkward stems.
- **Representative Examples Cited:** Das and Majumder (2017), Kaur and Singh (2017), Kusuma and Alhamri (2018), Adithya and Singh (2017).

#### **B. Semantic / Semantic-Based Approaches**

- **How It Works:** Requires a deeper conceptual understanding of the input beyond surface lexical and syntactic structures. It extracts explicit and implicit relations (e.g., _is-a_ class hierarchies, role relations) and applies domain reasoning, feature-based similarity, or contextual embeddings.
- **Required Inputs:** Structured knowledge sources (such as Web Ontologies [OWL] or Resource Description Framework [RDF] knowledge bases), or unstructured text supplemented by external semantic resources (e.g., WordNet, gazetteers, concept embeddings).
- **Strengths:** Supports a significantly wider variety of non-language domains (such as medicine, biology, history, and computer science); enables automated generation of plausible distractors via feature-based similarity or query relaxation; and allows questions to differ structurally from the raw input text.
- **Limitations:** Highly dependent on structured knowledge sources or rich semantic annotations, which are expensive to build and unavailable for many specialized educational domains.
- **Representative Examples Cited:** Alsubait et al. (2016), Leo et al. (2019) / Kurdi et al. (2019), Vinu and Kumar (2015b, 2017a), Seyler et al. (2017), Jouault et al. (2015a).

---

### **2. Dimension 2: Transformation Procedure (Question Formation Method)**

#### **A. Template-Based Approaches**

- **How It Works:** Generates questions using predefined surface structures consisting of fixed text and placeholders (slots). These placeholders are populated with values extracted from the input that satisfy specified syntactic or semantic constraints.
- **Required Inputs:** Handcrafted question templates paired with text or structured knowledge sources (ontologies/KBs).
- **Strengths:** Generates questions whose surface wording differs from the original input text (avoiding direct copy-pasting of input sentences); maintains strong grammatical fluency when templates are well-designed.
- **Limitations:** Manual template authoring is costly and time-consuming; generated question sets suffer from limited linguistic diversity and repetitive phrasing.
- **Representative Examples Cited:** Polozov et al. (2015), Alsubait et al. (2016), Mazidi and Nielsen (2015), Vinu and Kumar (2015b), Zavala and Mendoza (2018).

#### **B. Rule-Based Approaches**

- **How It Works:** Annotates input sentences with syntactic or semantic tags and matches them against handcrafted rule patterns. These rules dictate how to select interrogative _wh_-words and manipulate sentence structure to convert assertive statements into questions.
- **Required Inputs:** Unstructured text with syntactic/semantic annotations (e.g., dependency parse trees, POS patterns).
- **Strengths:** Offers explicit linguistic control over sentence transformation and question-type assignment.
- **Limitations:** Highly expensive to construct manually; struggles with complex, long, or non-standard sentence structures.
- **Representative Examples Cited:** Liu et al. (2017), Huang and He (2016), Chinkina and Meurers (2017), Flor and Riordan (2018).

#### **C. Statistical / Data-Driven Methods**

- **How It Works:** Learns question transformation and candidate selection automatically from training data. It frame question generation as a sequence-to-sequence prediction problem (using co-occurrence probabilities) or uses machine learning models to select gap positions or rank candidate distractors.
- **Required Inputs:** Large annotated textual corpora or question-answer datasets (e.g., SQuAD, NewsQA, QGSTEC).
- **Strengths:** Reduces reliance on manual rule creation or expert template design; scales effectively over large datasets.
- **Limitations:** Requires massive volumes of annotated training data, which are scarce or non-existent in specialized educational domains.
- **Representative Examples Cited:** Gao et al. (2018), Kumar et al. (2015b, 2018), Wang et al. (2018), Marrese-Taylor et al. (2018), Blšták and Rozinajová (2018).

# Understanding Input Sources
Based on the systematic review by Kurdi et al. (2020), here is a detailed breakdown of the input sources used in Automatic Question Generation (AQG), how unstructured text compares to structured knowledge sources, and the approaches most applicable to generating multiple-choice questions (MCQs) from lecture notes or PDFs.

---

### **1. Input Sources Used for Educational Question Generation**

According to the authors, AQG systems utilize three main categories of inputs:

- **Unstructured Text:** The most prevalent input source across the literature (used in 42 of the 72 reviewed studies post-2014 and 38 studies pre-2014). It includes textbook chapters, Wikipedia articles, reading comprehension passages, news articles, and web snippets.
- **Structured Knowledge Sources (Semantic Web):**
    - **Ontologies (OWL):** Utilized in 8 studies post-2014 (and 11 pre-2014) to represent domain concepts, axioms, and class hierarchies (_is-a_ relationships).
    - **Resource Description Framework (RDF) Knowledge Bases:** Utilized in 6 studies post-2014 (such as DBpedia or Freebase) containing subject-predicate-object triples.
- **Pre-existing Question Stems & Keys:** Utilized in 5 studies specifically to generate distractors for existing question stems and correct answers.
- **Other Specialized Formats:** Less common inputs include data tables, mathematical logic formulas, code snippets, or user-defined scenarios.

---

### **2. Unstructured Text vs. Structured Knowledge Sources**

The paper highlights several fundamental differences in how questions are constructed depending on whether the input is unstructured text or a structured knowledge base:

|Feature / Dimension|Unstructured Text Input|Structured Knowledge Sources (Ontologies / RDF)|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Primary Question Formats**|Favors **Free-Response (FR)** questions (21 studies) and **gap-fill / cloze** items.|Favors **Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)** (10 studies vs. 4 for free-response).|
|**Distractor Generation**|**Difficult.** Text does not inherently incorporate false statements or incorrect choices. Generators must rely on external dictionaries, POS matching, or contextual embeddings.|**Natural & Systematic.** Ontologies explicitly define sibling classes, property constraints, and hierarchies, allowing distractors to be queried directly via query relaxation or sibling extraction.|
|**Domain Coverage**|Heavy concentration in **language learning** (vocabulary, reading comprehension, grammar) and simple factual lookup.|Supports a broader variety of **non-language domains** (e.g., medicine, biology, history, computer science).|
|**Question Structural Variety**|Stems often closely mirror the exact surface wording of the source sentence.|Supports greater structural variety because abstract concepts and axioms can be mapped onto diverse verbalisation templates.|

---

### **3. Approaches Relevant to Generating MCQs from Lecture Notes or PDFs**

A professor's lecture notes or PDF documents fall under the category of **unstructured text**. According to the pipeline tasks and methods analyzed in the review, generating high-quality MCQs from text requires combining several specific approaches:

#### **A. Preprocessing & Content Selection**

1. **Sentence Simplification:** Lecture notes and textbook PDFs often contain long, complex sentences. Applying sentence simplification rules or parse-tree dependencies reduces complex statements into simple assertions to make concept extraction easier.
2. **Topic-Based Content Selection:** To identify which parts of the PDF deserve a question, systems employ topic modeling (e.g., LDA) or document embedding vector spaces to score and rank sentences based on their topical importance.

#### **B. Question Stem & Correct Key Construction**

- **Template-Based or Rule-Based Transformation:** The generator identifies key entities/concepts in a simplified sentence, removes the concept to form the correct answer (key), and converts the remaining clause into an interrogative stem using handcrafted rules or templates.

#### **C. Distractor Generation (The Text-MCQ Bottleneck Solution)**

- **Hybrid External Resource Coupling:** Because raw text lacks built-in distractors, the paper shows that successful text-based MCQ systems _must_ couple the input text with external structured resources.
- **Similarity-Based Distractor Selection:** Distractors are selected from external knowledge sources (such as WordNet, DBpedia, or concept embeddings) based on **contextual similarity**, **feature-based similarity**, or **Part-of-Speech (POS) matching** to ensure they are plausible yet unambiguously incorrect.

#### **D. Post-Processing & Verbalisation**

- **Linguistic Smoothing:** Applying coreference resolution (replacing ambiguous pronouns with explicit nouns) and determiner/verb adjustment ensures the stem reads naturally and avoids giving away grammatical clues.

### **4. Clear Distinction: Paper Evidence vs. Assistant Inference**

- **Evidence Directly Supported by the Paper:**
    
    - Text-based MCQ generation is inherently bottlenecked by distractor generation and requires external knowledge bases or embedding vectors.
    - Sentence simplification, topic modeling, coreference resolution, and similarity-based distractor selection are the standard pipeline tasks used for text processing.
    - Ontology-based QG naturally produces better MCQs than raw text alone.
- **Assistant Inference (My Recommendation for Practical Implementation):**
    
    - _Recommendation:_ If a professor wants to build an automated MCQ generator from raw PDF lecture notes, the most effective design architecture based on the review's findings would be a **two-tier hybrid system**:
        1. Use a **syntactic parse-and-simplify rule model** on the PDF text to extract important factual definitions and construct question stems.
        2. Feed the extracted key terms into a **pre-existing domain ontology or semantic embedding space** (e.g., DBpedia or WordNet) specifically to pull sibling concepts as distractors.
    - _Alternative Recommendation:_ If an external ontology is unavailable for the professor's specific course, the generator should default to **gap-fill (cloze) MCQs** where distractors are pulled from other topically similar sentences within the same PDF using POS-matching and sentence embeddings, as this minimizes ungrammatical outputs.

# Focus on MCQ Generation
In the systematic review by **Kurdi et al. (2020)**, Multiple-Choice Question (MCQ) generation represents one of the primary response formats evaluated across the literature (accounting for 31 studies pre-2014 and 35 studies post-2014).

Below is an analysis of how MCQ generation, key selection, and distractor generation are structured in the paper, alongside the key challenges, research gaps, and scope boundaries identified by the authors.

---

### **1. MCQ Generation Framework & Pipeline**

The authors divide the overall question-generation pipeline into three stages: **preprocessing**, **question construction**, and **post-processing**.

- **Input-Type Dynamics for MCQs:**
    - **Structured Knowledge Sources (Ontologies & RDF KBs):** These inputs naturally lend themselves to MCQ generation (10 out of 14 studies using Semantic Web technologies focused on MCQs) because domain concepts, class hierarchies (_is-a_ relations), and properties allow for systematic distractor extraction.
    - **Unstructured Text Inputs:** Text inputs primarily yield free-response or gap-fill items. When unstructured text is used for MCQs, external knowledge sources (e.g., WordNet, DBpedia, or concept embeddings) must be coupled with the text to supply plausible distractors, as raw text does not inherently contain false or alternative choices.
- **Transformation Procedures:** MCQ stems and items are formed using three main transformation procedures: **template-based** (filling fixed surface placeholders with values), **rule-based** (matching linguistic patterns to reorder sentences), or **statistical/data-driven** (learning sequence mappings or gap positions from data).

---

### **2. Answer-Generation Methods (Stem & Key Selection)**

Generating the question stem and correct answer (key) typically occurs simultaneously during the construction phase. The paper highlights several techniques for key selection and stem formation:

- **Gap-Fill / Cloze Stems:** The system selects key target terms (e.g., specific Part-of-Speech [POS] tags, named entities, or high-tf-idf concepts) from an input sentence and removes them to form a gap.
- **Wh-Question Stems:** The answer entity is extracted, a corresponding interrogative word (_what, where, who, when_) is assigned, and the remaining sentence structure is manipulated or mapped to a template.
- **Ontology-Based Axiom Selection:** Stems and keys are extracted directly from formal ontologies or RDF triples (e.g., selecting class definitions, domain properties, or subject-object relations as keys).

---

### **3. Distractor-Generation Techniques**

The authors emphasize that **distractor generation is the primary technical bottleneck in MCQ construction** because distractors directly govern the item's pedagogical quality and validity. The review identifies seven distinct distractor-generation strategies in the literature:

1. **Word Frequency & POS Matching:** Selecting candidates that match the correct key's POS tag or corpus frequency (e.g., Soonklang & Muangon 2017; Jiang & Lee 2017).
2. **Co-occurrence:** Selecting words that frequently co-occur with the key in a reference corpus (e.g., Jiang & Lee 2017).
3. **Similarity-Based Selection (The Dominant Approach):**
    - _Syntax-Based Similarity:_ Matching POS tags, letter lengths, or phonetic/spelling similarities (e.g., Satria & Tokunaga 2017a; Kumar et al. 2015b).
    - _Feature-Based Similarity:_ Computing similarity over entity attribute vectors or class hierarchies in ontologies (e.g., Alsubait et al. 2016; Majumder & Saha 2015; Patra & Saha 2018a; Leo et al. 2019).
    - _Contextual & Semantic Similarity:_ Utilizing word vector space embeddings (Word2Vec) or WordNet synsets to select topically close words (e.g., Afzal 2015; Yaneva et al. 2018; Shah et al. 2017).
4. **Ontology Sibling Extraction:** Querying knowledge bases for entities that share the same direct parent class (_siblings_) as the key under an _is-a_ hierarchy (e.g., Lopetegui et al. 2015; Faizan & Lohmann 2018; Susanti et al. 2015).
5. **Query Relaxation:** Relaxing the SPARQL or graph query constraints that extracted the key, thereby retrieving closely related entities that share some—but not all—attributes of the key (e.g., Seyler et al. 2017; Stasaski & Hearst 2017).
6. **Machine Learning & Learning-to-Rank:** Applying ML models (such as Generative Adversarial Networks or Learning-to-Rank models) to rank candidate distractors pulled from large corpora (e.g., Liang et al. 2017, 2018; Liu et al. 2018).
7. **Task-Specific & Syntactic Rules:** Enforcing negative constraints, such as ensuring distractors for pronoun reference items do not belong to the same coreference chain (Satria & Tokunaga 2017a), or testing whether distractors fit grammatically into a cloze sentence (Kumar et al. 2015b).

---

### **4. Reported Challenges and Limitations**

The authors report several major limitations in existing MCQ generation research:

- **Overly Simple & Low Cognitive Level:** Most generated MCQs target simple surface-level recall or short factual lookup rather than higher-order thinking (such as application, analysis, or evaluation on Bloom's taxonomy).
- **One-Dimensional Difficulty Models:** Existing difficulty controls focus almost exclusively on key-distractor similarity. The authors note that similarity is only one facet of difficulty; overall item difficulty is driven by the interaction between stem complexity and option dynamics.
- **Grammatical Clues & Verbalisation Flaws:** Mismatches in agreement (such as singular/plural determiners or verb tenses) between the stem and distractors often give away the answer or confuse knowledgeable students.
- **Unvalidated Distractor Functionality:** While many studies evaluate distractor "plausibility" via expert ratings, very few evaluate empirical **distractor functionality**—whether test-takers actually select the distractors in real exam settings (only 2 studies reported empirical distractor functionality).

---

### **5. Explicitly Described vs. Insufficiently Covered / Out-of-Scope Topics**

#### **A. Topics Insufficiently Covered in the Literature (Explicit Gaps Identified by the Authors)**

- **Feedback Generation for MCQs:** Feedback explaining _why_ a selected distractor is wrong is almost non-existent (only 1 study out of 72, Leo et al. 2019, generated explanatory feedback alongside MCQs).
- **Automated Template Construction:** MCQ templates remain heavily author-driven and handcrafted by domain experts, creating a bottleneck in question diversity and scaling.
- **Exam-Level Assembly:** Almost all studies focus on generating isolated single questions; assembling balanced, non-overlapping MCQ test forms remains unexplored.

#### **B. Topics Outside the Paper's Coverage**

- **Modern Large Language Models (LLMs):** Because this review covers research published from **2015 to early 2019**, generative LLMs (such as GPT-3.5, GPT-4, LLaMA, or zero-shot/few-shot prompting) postdate the study's review window and are **outside the scope of this paper**.

# Evaluation Methods
In their systematic literature review, **Kurdi et al. (2020)** examine how automatically generated educational questions are evaluated across recent literature. The authors find that evaluation practices are highly fragmented, heterogeneous, and frequently plagued by reporting deficiencies, making performance comparisons across systems impossible.

Below is an in-depth breakdown of the evaluation methods discussed in the review, categorized by **Human Evaluation**, **Automatic Evaluation Metrics**, **Dataset-Based Evaluation**, and **Other Methods**, along with their measurements, limitations, paper evidence, and explicit scope boundaries.

---

### **1. Human Evaluation Methods**

#### **A. Expert Review**

- **What it Measures:** Evaluates overall question quality, acceptability, grammatical correctness, semantic ambiguity, domain relevance, educational usefulness, distractor plausibility, and estimated difficulty.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Expert review is the single most common evaluation method across the reviewed literature, utilized in **21 studies**. It serves as a standard proxy for question quality in real exams and acts as an initial filter to remove invalid, ambiguous, or guessable items.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:**
    - Experts are rarely asked to _answer_ the questions during review, missing an opportunity to trigger deeper cognitive engagement and identify subtle ambiguities.
    - Reviewer instructions are frequently ambiguous (e.g., failing to clarify whether experts should read the source passage before rating).
    - Rating scales are unstandardized and overly granular (using up to 9-point scales without clear category definitions), leading to reviewer bias and low inter-rater agreement.
    - Reporting is flawed: 23 studies omitted expert demographic/teaching experience, and 51 studies failed to state whether reviewers were paid, obscuring potential study biases. Expert review is also expensive and time-consuming.

#### **B. Student Testing & Mock Exams**

- **What it Measures:** Measures empirical item difficulty (such as percentage correct or Rasch model difficulty), item discrimination indices (ability to differentiate high from low performers), real student performance, and pre/post-test learning outcome gains.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Employed in **10 studies** for test-taking and **9 studies** for student review. It is also used to validate difficulty prediction models across 7 studies.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:**
    - Low adoption rate because conducting student trials is time-consuming and involves ethical and instructional risks.
    - Experimental exams can inadvertently distort student study habits (e.g., over-generated topics might mislead students into assuming those topics are heavily weighted on official exams) or damage motivation if questions are inappropriately difficult or easy.
    - Sample sizes in student trials are typically small (most studies evaluated fewer than 100 questions on fewer than 30 students), limiting statistical generalizability.

#### **C. Crowdsourcing (e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk / AMT)**

- **What it Measures:** Solicits crowd-worker feedback on question readability, fluency, distractor plausibility, overall acceptability, or mock test completion.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Used in **10 studies** (8 for reviewing questions and 3 for taking mock tests).
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:**
    - Crowd-workers generally lack specialized domain expertise and are unsuitable for evaluating complex, domain-specific subjects such as advanced mathematics or medicine.
    - Most studies fail to validate worker reliability or include quality checks (such as inserting control questions to screen out careless responses).

---

### **2. Automatic Evaluation Metrics**

#### **A. Lexical / N-Gram Overlap Metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR)**

- **What it Measures:** Measures surface-level text overlap between machine-generated questions and human-authored reference questions generated from the same input sentence. **ROUGE** evaluates n-gram recall, **BLEU** measures precision, and **METEOR** incorporates stemming and synonymy matching.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Adapted from natural language translation and summarization fields and applied in text-based QG studies: ROUGE featured in **6 studies**, BLEU in **5 studies**, and METEOR in **3 studies**.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:**
    - These metrics provide a fast, low-cost indicator of basic surface fluency and relevance, but they only measure surface-structure similarity to a specific gold-standard question.
    - They completely fail to evaluate pedagogical utility, factual accuracy, or item difficulty. A generated question that uses valid, alternative phrasing or targets a different legitimate concept from the text will receive a low score simply because it does not match the exact n-grams of the reference question.

#### **B. Statistical Difficulty & Discrimination Metrics**

- **What it Measures:** Calculates quantitative item parameters from examinee response data, such as _Percentage Correct_ (p-value, where 0% represents maximum difficulty and 100% represents maximum easiness) or Item Discrimination Indices.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Statistical difficulty based on student performance was measured in **19 studies**, while item discrimination was evaluated in **5 studies**.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:**
    - Extreme measurement heterogeneity exists; some studies report percentage correct while others report Rasch difficulty parameters without publishing the raw data needed to convert between scales.
    - These metrics cannot be calculated _a priori_ and require administering test items to large student cohorts, which is rarely feasible during early system development.

---

### **3. Dataset-Based Evaluation**

- **What it Measures:** Assesses a generator’s ability to reproduce human-like questions or rank plausible distractors by benchmarking output against public corpora.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** The authors identify seven prominent question corpora utilized in AQG research:
    1. **SQuAD** (150K Wikipedia QA pairs) & **NewsQA** (120K CNN QA pairs): Used to calculate automated BLEU/ROUGE/METEOR overlap scores against paragraph-answer pairs.
    2. **30M Factoid Question-Answer Corpus**: Used for comparing Freebase triple-based factual questions.
    3. **SciQ** (13.7K science MCQs) & **MCQL** (7.1K web-crawled MCQs): Used to train and evaluate machine learning models for distractor ranking.
    4. **QGSTEC Dataset**: Used for benchmarking shared task sentence/paragraph question generation.
    5. **Medical CBQ Corpus** (435 case-based medical MCQs): Used for evaluating ontology-based generators and difficulty models.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:**
    - **Lack of Educational Relevance:** The majority of standard corpora (SQuAD, NewsQA, 30M Factoid) were created for training Machine Reading Comprehension or Question Answering systems, _not_ for educational assessment. Their educational value and pedagogical quality have never been validated.
    - **Unvalidated Distractor Quality:** Corpora containing human-authored distractors (like SciQ or MCQL) are not necessarily high quality, as literature demonstrates that human item writers frequently create non-functional distractors. Distractors in these datasets are rarely filtered based on empirical student response data.
    - **Low Cross-Study Adoption:** Most corpora are used by only 1 or 2 research groups because each dataset is tied to a specific domain or format.

---

### **4. Other Evaluation Methods Discussed**

#### **A. Human-Machine Indistinguishability (Turing-Style Tests)**

- **What it Measures:** Tests whether reviewers or experts can distinguish automatically generated questions from human-authored exam questions when presented in a mixed, randomized sample.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Applied in **6 studies**. For example, Chinkina & Meurers (2017) reported that 67% of machine-generated language questions were mistaken for human-authored items, while Susanti et al. reported 45%.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:** Demonstrating that a question appears "human-like" does not prove that it is educationally useful or pedagogically sound, as human-authored items themselves frequently suffer from structural flaws and surface-level focus.

#### **B. Coverage / Overlap with Human Teacher Questions**

- **What it Measures:** Measures the percentage of concepts, topics, or question structures generated by human teachers that an automated generator can successfully cover from the same text.
- **Evidence from the Paper:** Evaluated in **5 studies**. For example, Jouault et al. reported that their ontology-based generator covered 84% of historical knowledge topics tested by human instructors.
- **Limitations Identified by Authors:** Measures topic recall but provides no insight into the grammatical accuracy, option difficulty, or distractor functionality of the generated items.

---

### **5. Recommendations: Paper-Supported Proposals vs. Assistant Inference**

#### **Paper-Supported Recommendations (Explicitly Proposed by Kurdi et al.):**

1. **Standardized & Automatic Scoring Procedures:** Establish standard, well-defined scoring rubrics and develop automatic evaluation metrics that explicitly correlate with human ratings to eliminate scale heterogeneity.
2. **Mandatory Reporting Checklists:** Enforce experimental reporting checklists (covering participant counts, teaching experience, payment details, sample size justifications, and inter-rater reliability) prior to peer review.
3. **Answering-Based Expert Review:** Require expert reviewers to actually _answer_ generated test items during review and conduct pre-evaluation pilot tests to validate rating instructions.
4. **Empirical Distractor Functionality:** Evaluate distractors based on actual student response patterns (whether students actually select them during exams) rather than relying solely on expert plausibility ratings.
5. **Mining Real Human Exams:** Translate real, human-authored exam questions into machine-processable representations to benchmark generator output and train realistic difficulty models.

#### **Assistant Inference (Explicitly Labeled as My Own Suggestion):**

- _Inference:_ If you are building an evaluation pipeline for a new AQG system today, a cost-effective design architecture based on these findings would be a **three-tier sequential screening pipeline**:
    1. **Automated Structural & Syntactic Pre-Filter:** Run rule-based grammar checkers and coreference resolvers to automatically discard ungrammatical or truncated stems before showing items to humans.
    2. **Targeted Expert Review with Forced Answering:** Present filtered items to domain experts, requiring them to answer each item and rate item acceptability on a standardized 3-point scale (_Acceptable_, _Needs Minor Revisions_, _Unacceptable_).
    3. **Pilot Student Administration:** Administer accepted items to a small student cohort to calculate empirical distractor functionality (filtering out distractors selected by 0% of students) and verify difficulty predictions.

# Identify Quality Problems
In their systematic review, Kurdi et al. (2020) identify several critical challenges in ensuring the quality of automatically generated educational questions. Below are the reported quality issues, supported by evidence from the paper, along with their specific significance for an educational Multiple-Choice Question (MCQ) generation system:

---

### **1. Grammatical Correctness & Naturalness (Verbalisation)**

- **Reported Challenge:** Automatically generated questions frequently suffer from ungrammaticality, awkward phrasing, or syntactic mangling, particularly when generated via syntactic sentence transformations. For instance, early landmark studies cited in the review reported that 36.7% of automatically generated questions were rated as ungrammatical. In MCQ generation, verbalisation issues often create grammatical inconsistencies between the stem and the options.
- **Significance for an MCQ System:** Grammatical flaws directly ruin item validity in two ways:
    1. A grammatical mismatch between the question stem and the distractors provides an artificial syntactic clue, allowing test-takers to eliminate wrong choices without possessing domain knowledge.
    2. A grammatical mismatch between the stem and the _correct answer (key)_ confuses knowledgeable test-takers, preventing them from selecting the key even when they understand the material.

---

### **2. Meaningfulness & Semantic Ambiguity**

- **Reported Challenge:** Beyond surface grammar, generated stems or options can be nonsensical or semantically ambiguous (evaluated across 11 studies in the review). Ambiguity often arises when sentence transformation algorithms lack deep semantic understanding or fail to resolve coreferences (e.g., leaving ambiguous pronouns like "he" or "it" without context).
- **Significance for an MCQ System:** Ambiguous stems make it impossible to measure student mastery fairly. If an item can be interpreted in multiple ways, students miss the question due to vague phrasing rather than a lack of knowledge, destroying the test's measurement accuracy.

---

### **3. Relevance to Source Material (Content Selection)**

- **Reported Challenge:** Identifying which parts of a text or knowledge base warrant a question is a major bottleneck. Generators that lack explicit content selection mechanisms generate questions from trivial text segments, sentences containing orphan connectives (e.g., "Thus, this happens..."), or peripheral details.
- **Significance for an MCQ System:** An educational MCQ system must align with key learning objectives. If the system selects trivial content, the resulting MCQs waste testing time on irrelevant details rather than evaluating core course concepts.

---

### **4. Answer Correctness & Distractor Functionality**

- **Reported Challenge:** Distractor generation is identified as the single biggest technical hurdle in MCQ construction. The review notes that generated distractors are frequently either too easy to eliminate, factually correct (creating multiple correct answers), or completely non-functional. Only 2 out of 72 reviewed studies empirically tested **distractor functionality** (i.e., verifying whether students actually choose the distractors during real exams).
- **Significance for an MCQ System:**
    1. If a distractor is accidentally correct, knowledgeable students are unfairly penalized.
    2. If distractors are non-functional (so obvious that no student ever selects them), a 4-option MCQ effectively collapses into a 2-option or 1-option question, artificially inflating student scores through guessing.

---

### **5. Difficulty Control**

- **Reported Challenge:** Difficulty control is severely neglected across the literature (addressed by only 14 studies). Most existing models rely solely on key-distractor similarity, ignoring how stem complexity, solution steps, and option interactions contribute to overall difficulty.
- **Significance for an MCQ System:** Uncontrolled difficulty leads to items that are either inappropriately easy or impossibly hard. In educational settings:
    1. Inappropriately easy MCQs fail to discriminate between high and low performers.
    2. Inappropriately difficult MCQs damage student motivation and induce test anxiety.
    3. Without predictable item difficulty, systems cannot assemble balanced exam forms or power adaptive learning environments.

---

### **6. Feedback Generation**

- **Reported Challenge:** Generating explanatory feedback alongside questions is almost non-existent in the literature—only **one study** out of 72 (Leo et al., 2019) generated pedagogical feedback explaining _why_ an option was right or wrong.
- **Significance for an MCQ System:** In modern e-learning and self-assessment environments (like MOOCs), an MCQ without feedback acts merely as a testing tool. Without explanatory feedback, generated MCQs fail to correct student misconceptions, explain wrong choices, or reinforce core learning concepts.

---

### **7. Question Structure & Cognitive Depth**

- **Reported Challenge:** The vast majority of generated questions are simple gap-fill (cloze) or factual _wh_-questions targeting lower cognitive levels (simple recall/memorization). Furthermore, because template construction is largely manual and costly, systems suffer from low linguistic diversity and repetitive structural patterns.
- **Significance for an MCQ System:** Simple recall MCQs fail to evaluate higher-order cognitive skills (such as application, analysis, or synthesis on Bloom's taxonomy). Repetitive template structures allow students to recognize patterns and guess answers without truly mastering the domain content.

---

# Research Gaps
Based on the authors' discussion and conclusion in **"A Systematic Review of Automatic Question Generation for Educational Purposes"** (Kurdi et al., 2020), here are the research gaps explicitly identified in the paper:

---

### **1. Lack of Controlled Question Difficulty**

- **(1) What is missing or inadequate:** Minimal emphasis on predicting, controlling, or manipulating question difficulty to generate test items of targeted difficulty levels.
- **(2) Evidence from the paper:** Only **14 out of 72 studies** addressed question difficulty. Existing models evaluated distractor similarity in isolation rather than assessing holistic item difficulty (the interaction between stem complexity, solution steps, and option choices), and models were validated on very small samples (fewer than 100 questions and 30 students).
- **(3) Why it matters for educational MCQ generation:** Uncontrolled difficulty produces MCQs that are either trivially easy (failing to differentiate student ability) or impossibly hard (inducing test anxiety). Predictable difficulty is required for adaptive testing platforms and assembling balanced exam papers.
- **(4) Does this review prove it remains unresolved in 2026?** **No.** This review analyzed literature published up to March 2019. While it provides empirical evidence that difficulty control was unresolved through 2019, it cannot confirm whether research conducted between 2019 and 2026 has resolved this issue.

---

### **2. Absence of Pedagogical Feedback Generation**

- **(1) What is missing or inadequate:** Almost complete lack of automated mechanisms to generate explanatory, formative, or summative feedback detailing _why_ a specific answer choice is correct or incorrect.
- **(2) Evidence from the paper:** Only **1 study out of 72** (Leo et al., 2019) generated pedagogical feedback alongside questions (by verbalizing underlying ontology axioms); one other study (Das & Majumder, 2017) provided syntactic hints.
- **(3) Why it matters for educational MCQ generation:** Without explanatory feedback, an MCQ functions strictly as an assessment tool rather than a learning instrument. Explanatory feedback is vital in self-directed e-learning and MOOCs to correct misconceptions when a student selects a distractor.
- **(4) Does this review prove it remains unresolved in 2026?** **No.** The paper's dataset ends in early 2019 and does not reflect post-2019 developments in natural language generation or educational technology.

---

### **3. Low Cognitive Depth & Lack of Structural Diversity**

- **(1) What is missing or inadequate:** Over-reliance on simple gap-fill (cloze) or factual _wh_-questions targeting lower-order recall (memorization), with little exploration of higher-order cognitive levels (e.g., Bloom’s taxonomy: application, analysis, evaluation). Additionally, template construction remains heavily manual.
- **(2) Evidence from the paper:** The vast majority of studies generated simple factual or cloze questions; 27 studies relied on manual templates and 16 on rigid syntactic rules, while automated template generation was virtually unstudied.
- **(3) Why it matters for educational MCQ generation:** Simple recall MCQs fail to evaluate complex domain understanding or problem-solving skills. Furthermore, repetitive template structures allow students to guess answers through surface pattern recognition rather than genuine subject mastery.
- **(4) Does this review prove it remains unresolved in 2026?** **No.** The review demonstrates that this was a major limitation in the 2015–2019 literature window, but an updated literature review would be required to verify its status in 2026.

---

### **4. Unstandardized & Methodologically Flawed Evaluation Practices**

- **(1) What is missing or inadequate:** Absence of unified evaluation standards, widespread reliance on translation/summarization NLP metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) that measure surface n-gram overlap rather than educational quality, extreme rating scale heterogeneity, and severe omissions in empirical reporting.
- **(2) Evidence from the paper:** Quality assessment (Q1–Q9 checklist) revealed that **43 out of 72 studies** failed to report inter-rater reliability, scale sizes varied wildly across studies, and only **2 studies** empirically evaluated **distractor functionality** (actual student selection rates during exams).
- **(3) Why it matters for educational MCQ generation:** Without standardized evaluation rubrics and empirical distractor testing, MCQ generators cannot be reliably benchmarked, compared, or validated for classroom adoption.
- **(4) Does this review prove it remains unresolved in 2026?** **No.** The 2020 review establishes that evaluation was a bottleneck up through 2019, but does not track evaluation frameworks developed after 2019.

---

### **5. Flaws in Natural Language Generation (Verbalisation)**

- **(1) What is missing or inadequate:** Insufficient application of Natural Language Generation (NLG) techniques to produce fluent, naturally phrased question stems and options, leading to awkward phrasing or grammatical errors.
- **(2) Evidence from the paper:** Only **10 studies** explicitly addressed verbalisation or naturalness post-2014; syntax-based transformations frequently yielded high ungrammaticality rates (up to 36.7% in landmark studies cited).
- **(3) Why it matters for educational MCQ generation:** Grammatical mismatches between an MCQ stem and its distractors provide artificial elimination clues, allowing students to guess the correct key without domain knowledge, or confusing students if the key itself is awkwardly phrased.
- **(4) Does this review prove it remains unresolved in 2026?** **No.** The paper's search cutoff was March 2019 and does not evaluate generative language models developed after this period.

---

### **6. Single-Item Generation vs. Exam-Level Assembly & Real-World Deployment**

- **(1) What is missing or inadequate:** Systems focus almost exclusively on generating isolated, standalone questions rather than assembling balanced, non-overlapping exam forms. Furthermore, real-world classroom deployment is almost completely absent from the literature.
- **(2) Evidence from the paper:** Only **1 study out of 72** (Zavala & Mendoza, 2018) reported actual deployment in a classroom setting; zero studies evaluated automated full-exam assembly algorithms.
- **(3) Why it matters for educational MCQ generation:** Instructors require complete, balanced test forms that cover an entire syllabus without redundant items, rather than loose collections of uncurated single MCQs.
- **(4) Does this review prove it remains unresolved in 2026?** **No.** While the paper proves this was an unaddressed gap through 2019, it provides no data on research conducted between 2020 and 2026.
